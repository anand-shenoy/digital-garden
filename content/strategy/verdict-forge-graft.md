# Verdict, Forge, Graft

Three ways to put an idea under pressure, and a test for knowing which one you are in.

I have been running ideas past an adversarial panel for a while. An advocate, a sceptic, and a referee who has to actually decide rather than summarise. It works. What I had not noticed until recently is that I had been using it for two completely different jobs without distinguishing between them, and there was a third I had invented by accident and never named.

## The test

One question decides it.

**Is the thing fixed, or can I still change it?**

That is all. Everything else follows.

## Verdict

**For someone else's artefact.** A repository, an article, a podcast transcript, a tool, a talk.

You cannot redesign it. It exists, it is finished, and it was made by someone whose situation is not yours. So the panel's job is to rule: which of these claims survives contact with what I am actually doing?

The discipline that makes it work is extracting the specific, falsifiable claims first and numbering them. Not themes, claims. *Uses AI agents to replace a twenty-person ops team* is a claim. *Is bullish on AI* is not. Every persona then argues about the same numbered list, which stops the advocate and the sceptic talking past each other.

Then every claim gets a verdict. Adopt, adapt, defer, or reject. Defer requires a stated trigger condition, otherwise it is rejection wearing a disguise. *Defer until the site has paying subscribers* is a trigger. *Revisit later* is not.

The value is entirely in the ruling. An analysis that ends "there are good points on both sides" has failed at the only thing it was for.

## Forge

**For your own idea, while it is still soft.**

Here the usual order is wrong. Normally you have an idea, you develop it, you put it in front of the panel, you get objections, you rework. The hard thinking happens after the criticism, under the slight defensiveness of having your idea taken apart.

Invert it. Read the panel's standing objections first, and use them as design constraints while you are still building. The hard thinking moves before the criticism instead of after it. Same rigour, better mood, and that is not trivial.

One rule keeps it honest, and without it the whole thing becomes self-deception:

**Fix the weakness. Never pre-write the rebuttal.**

Anticipating an objection and arming a defence is teaching to the test. You end up mistaking a dodged question for a solved problem. Changing the design is different, and it is the only version worth doing. If you notice yourself drafting an argument rather than altering the thing, stop.

The tell that it is working is strange and worth watching for. The questions stop feeling like objections and start feeling like feedback, the sort you get when you have a product and you are talking to people about it.

The tell that it is failing is the same feeling for the opposite reason. If nothing ever feels like an objection, the questions are probably being chosen for comfort.

**Two limits worth knowing before you rely on it.**

It puts a floor under an idea. It does not raise the ceiling. On the first time I ran it deliberately, the strongest thing in the resulting product did not come from answering any objection at all. It came from my own experience of the subject, when I was asked about an edge case. The method sharpens what you bring. It does not supply it.

And the panel cannot genuinely surprise you when the same mind designed the idea and ran the panel. It is a stress test, not an outside verdict. Someone who was not in the room will still find things you could not.

## Graft

**For when you liked someone else's thing and want your own version.**

Verdict on theirs, then Forge yours on only what survived.

The name is horticultural and exact. You take a cutting from someone else's plant and grow it on your own rootstock. What you do not do is plant the whole thing in your garden and hope it likes the soil.

This is the strongest of the three, because it separates two jobs that get muddled constantly. **Verdict is for input. Forge is for output.** The panel tells you which of someone else's claims survive contact with your situation, and then you build your own thing on top of those, and only those.

The failure mode is grafting onto rejected wood. Anything that came back as reject, or as a defer with no trigger, is not rootstock. It is the bit you were supposed to cut away.

## When to use none of them

When the idea is cheap to test.

The first time I ran Forge properly it produced eight verdicts and a written specification, and its own correct next action was still: build one screen and see whether it is any fun. No amount of structured argument could answer that. No amount ever will.

A weekend beats an hour of excellent reasoning more often than anyone running a process would like to admit.

## Three secondary tests

When the first question is ambiguous, these break the tie.

**Cost to test.** If building it answers the question faster than arguing about it, build it.

**Your own attachment.** If you have already decided you want to do this, Verdict's adversarial structure protects you from yourself. Forge becomes elaborate self-justification when your mind is made up, and it will feel like progress the entire way.

**The shape of the question.** *Should I adopt this* is Verdict. *What should this be* is Forge.
